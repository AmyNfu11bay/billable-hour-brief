What One More Billable Hour Is Worth

Example Shop  ·  An operating brief for shop ownership  ·  Fullbay onboarding & enablement program

This shop's revenue is completed, billable hours — not bays, not headcount. One more of those per technician per week is worth $21,420 a year in gross profit, and it takes no new customer, no new hire and no new equipment.

Half an hour a week is about six minutes a day per technician. At 4 technicians, one hour each is $428 of gross profit a week — $1,855 a month.

Where the hour actually comes from

  • Time clocked in but not on a job. Usually waiting on parts, waiting on a hand-off, or an hour that never got clocked — rarely effort.
  • Jobs that finish at ninety percent. An unfinished job earns nothing this week, however much work went into it.
  • Comebacks. The same hour spent twice, and typically worth more than the original job earned.
  • Work already in the bay. Inspections turn up a safety issue 60% of the time and 89% of those get repaired. It needs no marketing.

What ownership should be deciding

Is the gap capacity or demand?If techs are clocked in but not on jobs, adding a technician buys a more expensive version of the same week.
When did we last move the labor rate?The industry median went $135 to $149 in under two years, and 12% of shops have never reassessed. A rate move is margin that requires nobody to work harder.
How fast does finished work become cash?Shops average 10.9 to 15.3 days from job open to invoice. Days sitting in Ready to Invoice are wages and parts already spent and not yet billed.
Can we keep the technicians we have?57% of shops are understaffed and 26% of techs intend to leave or start their own shop within a year. A replacement hire costs more than a raise.

Using this for raises and promotions

What this data supports
  • Sustained performance — a rolling three to six months, not one good week.
  • Certification tier reached — an earned, documented standard applied the same way to everyone.
  • Quality alongside output — comebacks read next to hours, so speed alone never carries a case.
  • Contribution in dollars — what the technician's year is worth, which is the language a raise conversation needs.
What it does not support
  • A single week, or a single rank. Rank moves weekly and partly reflects which jobs got assigned.
  • Anything the technician doesn't control. Utilization suffers when parts and dispatch are slow; that isn't a pay issue.
  • Denying a raise by itself. Use it to justify a yes, and to show what a yes would require — not as evidence for no.

A ladder that works: Basic certification qualifies, Advanced plus two sustained quarters earns senior, Master plus mentoring earns lead. Confirm any pay change with HR and payroll before it reaches a technician.

Assumptions. Contribution per hour is the labor rate less the technician's loaded hourly cost, on the basis that fixed overhead is covered by current volume. Annualized at 50 working weeks.
